How is r calculated? Follow the vectors and their angle

This walkthrough follows the observations in the scatterplot. Start with a small example to see every number, then use the same steps with any data.

These buttons change the actual paired observations above. Reset restores the original example.

1 · Read each column as one vector

Each dot in the scatterplot is one observation with two values. Now read down the x column to make one list, and down the y column to make another. A vector is this ordered list viewed as an arrow. Keep the observations in the same order in both lists.

x=(x1,x2,…,xn),y=(y1,y2,…,yn)

Multiply matching components and add the products to get the dot product. Its size depends on both direction and length, so it is not yet Pearson’s r.

The diagram below represents the two whole lists as arrows. Each arrow has one component per observation. Two arrows from the same origin lie in a shared plane: drawing that plane preserves their angle, even when they have many components.

2 · Measure deviations from each mean

Subtract the x mean from every x value and the y mean from every y value. Call the new vectors a and b. A positive component now means “above this variable’s mean”; a negative component means “below its mean”.

ai=xi−x¯,bi=yi−y¯

When both deviations have the same sign, their product is positive. Opposite signs give a negative product. Their sum measures whether the two variables tend to deviate together.

Centred values for the first 30 observations
x y a = x − x̄ b = y − ȳ a × b

Try adding 10 to every y. Its mean also increases by 10, so its deviations stay the same. The raw vectors can change angle; the centred vectors do not.

3 · Keep direction and set each length to 1

Find each centred vector’s length: square its components, add them, then take the square root.

Lx=∑ai2,Ly=∑bi2

Divide every component by its own vector’s length. The resulting unit vectors u and v have length 1. They keep the directions of a and b, so the angle stays the same.

ui=aiLx,vi=biLy

Unit length means the squared components add to 1. It differs from setting the sample standard deviation to 1.

Try multiplying every y by 2. Its centred vector doubles in length. Dividing by that new length returns the same unit vector.

4 · The remaining similarity is an angle

The dot product of two arrows equals the product of their lengths times the cosine of their angle. Once both lengths are 1, their dot product is just the cosine. For our centred vectors, this is Pearson’s r.

r=a·bLxLy=u·v=cosθ

The dotted line drops v onto u. The green segment shows how much the unit vector v points along u. That signed amount is r: towards u is positive; away from u is negative.

Reference angles between centred vectors
Angle r = cos θ Directions
0° +1 Same
60° +0.5 Partly aligned
90° 0 Perpendicular
120° −0.5 Partly opposed
180° −1 Opposite

Adding a constant or multiplying by a positive number preserves r. Multiplying one variable by a negative number reverses its direction and flips the sign of r.

Connect this to the familiar Pearson formula
r=∑(xi−x¯)(yi−y¯)∑(xi−x¯)2⋅∑(yi−y¯)2

The numerator is the centred dot product. The denominator is the product of the two centred vector lengths. The usual sample-covariance and sample-SD formula gives the same result because its n − 1 factors cancel.

Model, assumptions & interpretation

The slider constructs a set of points with the selected Pearson r; it does not simulate a population correlation or a hypothesis test. New pattern rearranges the constructed points while keeping r fixed. Manual input calculates r from your own paired observations. A value of zero means no linear association, but other relationships can still exist. Outliers can strongly affect r, and correlation does not imply causation.
